Writing a pun takes 5 steps: pick a topic, build a word bank, find sound-alikes and double meanings, write the line, and test it out loud. Follow them in order and you'll end an original pun, not a recycled one — this guide builds one example pun, on coffee, all the way through.

## Step 1: Pick a Topic

Pick one specific topic rather than a broad category — "coffee" works better than "drinks," because a narrow topic gives you a smaller, more specific word bank to pull from in the next step. For this guide, the topic is coffee.

## Step 2: Build a Word Bank

List every word you associate with your topic, including its objects, actions, and slang. For coffee, that word bank includes: brew, bean, roast, grounds, espresso, latte, steep, perk, drip, and cup. The bigger the word bank, the more raw material you have to find a double meaning in.

## Step 3: Find Sound-Alikes and Double Meanings

Go through the word bank one word at a time and ask whether it sounds like another word, or whether it already carries a second meaning. "Espresso" sounds like "express," "perk" means both to brighten up and a feature of a job, and "grounds" means both coffee sediment and a legal justification. These are the two mechanisms behind every pun — see [homophonic puns](/homophonic-puns/) for the sound-alike version and [homographic puns](/homographic-puns/) for the double-meaning version.

## Step 4: Write the Line

Build a short sentence that uses the word naturally in its pun context while still making sense in its original meaning. From "grounds," you get "My coffee has grounds for a promotion" — the sentence works both as a coffee statement and a workplace one. From "perk," you get "This coffee perks me up every morning," underlining the double role the word plays.

## Step 5: Test It Out Loud

Say the line out loud to someone else before you consider it finished — a pun that reads fine on the page sometimes doesn't land when spoken, especially sound-alike puns where the rhythm matters. If the listener has to ask for an explanation, the pun needs another pass.

## What Makes a Pun Good vs. Bad?

A good pun lands the instant it's heard, without needing the listener to stop and work it out — "my coffee has grounds for a promotion" gets a laugh immediately because both meanings are equally clear. A bad pun forces the connection, like "my coffee is very bean-eficial," where the wordplay is stretched too far to feel natural. See [Bad Puns](/bad-puns/) for examples where the forced, groan-on-purpose version is actually the goal.
